1.3 Arguments vs Explanations 
Arguments try to convince that some conclusion is true. Explanations show why a conclusion is true. Explanations tend to presuppose that a conclusion is already taken for granted.
1.4 More complex argument structures
Main conclusion: a statement (conclusion) that depends on other statements, but other statements do not depend on it.
Intermediate conclusion: a statement (conclusion) that depends on other states, and other statements (conclusion) depend on it. These statements can be both premises and conclusions.
Similarly, we have main arguments and sub arguments.
Example:
1. The lava from Mt. Vesuvius was flowing too fast and there was nowhere for someone living in Pompeii to go in order to escape it in time. 
2. Therefore, no one living in Pompeii could have survived the eruption of Mt. Vesuvius. (from 1) 
3. Therefore, this account of the eruption of Mt. Vesuvius was not actually written by an eyewitness. (from 2)

1.6 Validity
A valid argument is an argument whose conclusion cannot possibly be false, assuming that the premises are true.

1. EveryoneborninFrancecanspeakFrench 2. BarackObamawasborninFrance 3. Therefore, Barack Obama can speak French (from 1-2)

1. GeorgewasPresidentoftheUnitedStates 2. Therefore, George was elected President of the United States (from 1)

2.15 Venn Diagram test of validity for immediate categorical inferences
If the Venn for the premise is the same as the Venn for the conclusion, then all the information in the conclusion is already contained in the premise, so it is valid.
If the conclusion contains information that is not included in the premise, then the argument is invalid.
Compare:
Some S are P. Therefore, some P are S. (valid)
All S are P. Therefore all P are S. (invalid)

3.7 The base rate fallacy
A test comes back positive that you have colon cancer. The test is 95% accurate, i.e. it will detect people with colon cancer 95% of the time, and miss 5%. 
sensitivity - it is about true positives vs false negatives
specificity - it is about true negatives vs few false positives
A 95% also means it will get 95% of the negative cases right, but will have 5% false negatives.
Fallacy of ignoring the base rate: the chance of you having colon cancer is 95%.
When true conditions are rare, even highly accurate tests should only raise the probability a little bit.
Base rate of colon cancer: 0.5%
So for 100,000 people, 500 have colon cancer.
Of the 500, the test will correctly identify 475. (The true positives.)
Of the 100,000 people, 99,500 do not have colon cancer.
Of the 99,500, the 95% accurate test will misdiagnose 5% of them, i.e. 4975 will be false positives.
So, your chances of having colon cancer given a positive test is 475/total # of positive identifications = 475/(475+4975) = 475/5450 = 0.087 = 8.7%.
I like to do this as a table:

Without information from the measurement/test, your chance of colon cancer is Base Rate * N / N
With information from the measure and supposing it’s positive, your chance is Accuracy * Base Rate.
NOTE: a true positive is a conjunction: the test says yes and the ground truth is yes
Another example: eyewitness testimony
Bob’s accuracy might be 99%. He says Nancy is the robber.
Suppose in 1000 people, 995 are not robbers and 5 are.
Bob will identify 4.9 robbers correctly, and misidentify 9.95 (false positives) (a total of 14.9 robber identification, i.e. true +false positives)
This gives 4.95 / 14.9 = 33%. It’s more likely Nancy is NOT the robber (absent more specific information).

4.1.3 Begging the question

Notice: the premise and conclusion are nearly identical.

4.3 Fallacies of relevance
Common theme: ways of responding to an argument that are irrelevant to the argument. 
The responses may be psychologically (or emotionally) compelling, but upon more careful analysis we recognize they are not rationally compelling.

4.3.1 Ad hominem
means “against the man”
- occurs when a response addresses the person and not the argument. 
Example: Peter Singer makes an argument that it is morally wrong to spend money on luxuries for oneself instead of giving that money away to charity. This is an argument by analogy (like we’ve seen before).
Now consider this criticism: Does Peter Singer give his money to charity?
Notice: this criticism is not about the argument, it’s about Peter Singer.
We should be interested in the quality of the argument.
That said, sometimes criticizing a person is a means of addressing an argument, e.g. witness testimony.
The question to ask: is the person relevant to the validity of the argument? If no, then it might be an ad hominem.

4.3.2 Straw man
Misrepresenting a position so as to make it easier to refute.
Is the critic’s reconstructed argument, B, that has been refuted with criticism C, relevant to the original argument or position, A.
DRAW THIS: C is a criticism of B. But if B is not a fair representation of A, i.e. if B is a “straw man”, then C is not relevant as a criticism to A.

4.3.3 Tu quoque
“you too” or appeal to hypocrisy
Avoiding a criticism by pointing out it applies to the criticer.

4.3.4 Genetic fallacy
	1.	Religious Belief:
	•	Person A: “I believe in practicing kindness and compassion because it’s a central teaching in my religion.”
	•	Person B: “Well, that’s just because you were raised in a religious family. If you hadn’t been, you wouldn’t believe that.”
	•	Here, Person B dismisses Person A’s belief by pointing to its origin (the religious upbringing), rather than addressing whether kindness and compassion are valid or valuable principles.
	2.	Scientific Discovery:
	•	Scientist A: “The theory of relativity has profound implications for our understanding of time and space.”
	•	Person B: “That theory came from Albert Einstein, who wasn’t even a ‘proper’ academic at the time he developed it, so why should we trust it?”
	•	This is the genetic fallacy because Person B dismisses the theory of relativity based on Einstein’s unconventional academic status rather than evaluating the scientific merits of the theory itself.
	3.	Political Argument:
	•	Person A: “I think this new policy proposal is beneficial for improving the economy.”
	•	Person B: “Of course you think that—you’re a member of that political party!”
	•	Instead of examining the proposal on its own merits, Person B discredits it by attributing Person A’s support solely to their party affiliation, implying that the proposal is bad simply because of its source.

Genetic fallacies are most common when the origins are negative, but they can also occur from positive origins.

4.3.5 Appeal to consequences
Like the genetic fallacy in reverse: the consequences of accepting an idea are not relevant to the truth of the idea.
Example: suppose a study reveals that there are IQ differences between sexes. A criticism of the study that focuses on the downstream consequences of accepting the results of the study is committing the appeal to consequences fallacy. A relevant criticism would be one that focuses on, e.g. a flaw in the design of the study (e.g. low sample size!).
Important: the researchers might choose not to publish the study because of it’s potential negative consequences. That would NOT be a fallacy. ASK WHY

Dennett's General Thinking Tools with Examples
Chapter II of Dennett's book Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking presents twelve general thinking tools. Here are the main concepts and examples for each of them:
1. Making Mistakes
Don't be afraid to make mistakes, as long as you learn from them. Dennett argues that mistakes are valuable opportunities for learning and growth. [1] He suggests that we should cultivate a habit of examining our mistakes to understand where we went wrong and how to avoid repeating them. [1]
Example: Imagine you are learning to play a new musical instrument. You are bound to make mistakes along the way, hitting wrong notes and struggling with difficult passages. [1] However, by paying attention to these mistakes and figuring out how to correct them, you will gradually improve your skills.
2. "By Parody of Reasoning": Using Reductio ad Absurdum
Reductio ad absurdum is a powerful technique for exposing the flaws in an argument by demonstrating that its logical consequences are ridiculous or impossible. [2]
Example: Someone argues that all beliefs are equally valid because everyone has a right to their own opinion. You could use reductio ad absurdum to counter this argument by pointing out that this would mean the belief that the earth is flat is equally valid as the belief that the earth is round, which is absurd.
3. Rapoport's Rules 
These rules are a set of guidelines for engaging in constructive dialogue and debate, particularly when discussing controversial topics. [3] They encourage respectful communication, careful listening, and an attempt to understand opposing viewpoints. [3]
Example: Before criticizing someone's argument, you should accurately restate their position in a way they would agree with, demonstrating that you have genuinely attempted to understand their perspective. [3]

Expanding on Rapoport's Rules
Rapoport's Rules are a set of guidelines for creating a successful critical commentary, especially when disagreeing with someone. They were developed by Anatol Rapoport, a social psychologist and game theorist. Dennett highlights these rules in his book as a powerful tool for fostering constructive dialogue and promoting understanding, even amidst disagreements [1]. They are particularly useful when discussing complex or controversial subjects, where emotions can run high and hinder productive conversations [1].
Here are the four rules as outlined by Dennett [2]:
Restate Your Target's Position: Begin by attempting to re-express your target's position so clearly, vividly, and fairly that they would say, "Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way" [2]. This demonstrates a genuine effort to understand and accurately represent their viewpoint before offering any criticism.
Highlight Points of Agreement: Next, list any points of agreement, especially if they are not matters of general or widespread agreement [2]. This helps establish common ground and shows that you are not simply engaging in blind opposition.
Acknowledge What You Have Learned: Mention anything you have learned from your target [2]. This further demonstrates your willingness to engage with their ideas and acknowledge their intellectual contributions.
Finally, Offer Rebuttal or Criticism: Only after completing the first three steps are you permitted to say so much as a word of rebuttal or criticism [2].
By following these rules, you can create a more receptive audience for your criticisms. You have already demonstrated your understanding of their position, your respect for their intellect, and your willingness to engage in good faith [2]. This approach can help foster a more constructive and productive dialogue, even when you fundamentally disagree with the other person.
Dennett points out that following Rapoport's Rules is not always easy, especially when dealing with arguments you find particularly frustrating or poorly constructed [3]. It can be tempting to simply dismiss or ridicule opposing viewpoints. However, he emphasizes the value of striving for fairness and accuracy, even when it is challenging [3].
Dennett shares his own experience using Rapoport's Rules when engaging with philosopher Robert Kane's views on free will. He diligently attempted to present a fair and comprehensive account of Kane's arguments, even though he ultimately disagreed with them [3]. This approach earned him respect from Kane, who appreciated Dennett's effort to accurately portray his position [3, 4].
Dennett concludes by recommending Rapoport's Rules as a valuable tool for anyone seeking to engage in thoughtful and productive debates. He acknowledges that it can be a struggle to follow these rules consistently, but emphasizes the rewarding results they can yield [4]. By prioritizing understanding and respect, Rapoport's Rules can help transform confrontational arguments into opportunities for genuine intellectual exchange and growth.



4. Sturgeon's Law
This adage states that "ninety percent of everything is crap." [4] Dennett uses it to remind us that most things in any given field are likely to be mediocre or worse. [4] It encourages us to be discerning and focus on the truly exceptional. [4]
Example: If you are browsing a new music streaming service, you might find that the vast majority of the songs are uninspired or derivative. Sturgeon's Law suggests that this is to be expected and that your time would be better spent seeking out the truly innovative and high-quality artists.
5. Occam's Razor
This principle states that, when faced with multiple explanations, the simplest one is usually the best. [5] It encourages us to avoid unnecessary complexity and prioritize parsimonious explanations. [5]
Example: You hear a strange noise in your attic. One explanation is that it's a ghost, while another is that it's a family of squirrels that has taken up residence. Applying Occam's Razor, the simpler explanation - squirrels - is more likely to be correct.
6. Occam's Broom
This satirical term refers to the practice of selectively ignoring evidence that contradicts one's preferred theory. [6] It is a warning against confirmation bias and intellectual dishonesty. [6]
Example: A climate change denier might dismiss overwhelming scientific evidence supporting the reality of human-caused climate change as "fake news" or a "conspiracy" to maintain funding for environmental research, effectively sweeping inconvenient facts under the rug.
7. Using Lay Audiences as Decoys
This "tool" is a criticism of the practice of simplifying complex ideas to the point of distortion in order to make them more palatable to a general audience. [7] Dennett argues that this can lead to the spread of misinformation and a superficial understanding of important issues. [7]
Example: A popular science book might oversimplify quantum mechanics by using misleading analogies or omitting crucial details, ultimately giving readers a flawed understanding of the subject.
8. Jootsing
This term, coined by Hofstadter, refers to the process of "jumping out of the system" to gain a fresh perspective on a problem. [8] It involves breaking free from conventional patterns of thought and exploring new approaches. [8]
Example: You are trying to solve a difficult math problem but keep getting stuck using the same approach. Jootsing would involve stepping back, trying to reframe the problem in a different way, and exploring alternative methods for finding a solution.
9. Three Species of Goulding (see below for expanded notes)
This refers to three rhetorical techniques often employed by the evolutionary biologist Stephen Jay Gould, which Dennett identifies as "rathering," "piling on," and the "Gould Two-Step." [9] These techniques, while sometimes effective, can be used to subtly mislead or misrepresent opposing viewpoints. [9]
Example: "Rathering" involves presenting a weaker, easily refutable version of an opponent's argument instead of engaging with their actual position. [9]
10. The "Surely" Operator: A Mental Block
Dennett observes that the word "surely" is often used to introduce a claim that the author wants the reader to accept without further argument. [10] He warns that this can be a sign of intellectual laziness or an attempt to smuggle in a questionable assumption. [10]
Example: A philosopher might write, "Surely, it is self-evident that consciousness cannot be explained by purely material processes." The use of "surely" here attempts to shut down critical thinking and encourages the reader to accept the claim uncritically. [10]
11. Rhetorical Questions
Dennett argues that rhetorical questions can be used to subtly bias an audience by embedding hidden assumptions or framing an issue in a particular way. [11]
Example: Asking "Why are liberals so obsessed with political correctness?" implies that liberals are indeed "obsessed" with political correctness and frames the issue as a problem rather than a positive attempt to promote respectful and inclusive language.
12. What Is a Deepity?
Dennett uses this term to describe statements that sound profound but are actually either trivially true or demonstrably false. [12] They often rely on ambiguity or vagueness to create an illusion of depth. [12]
Example: "Love is just a word." This statement might sound insightful at first, but on closer examination, it is either trivially true (words are just labels for concepts) or demonstrably false (love is a complex emotion with profound psychological and behavioral effects).
These twelve tools, along with the other concepts discussed throughout Intuition Pumps, provide a framework for thinking more critically and effectively about a wide range of topics.

Three species of Goulding - expanded.
Dennett's "Three Species of Goulding"
As described in the previous response, the "Three Species of Goulding" are rhetorical techniques that philosopher Daniel Dennett identifies in the works of evolutionary biologist Stephen Jay Gould. These techniques can be effective in argumentation, but Dennett warns that they can also be used to subtly mislead or misrepresent opposing viewpoints. He calls them "species of Goulding" as a playful nod to Gould's expertise in taxonomy and evolutionary biology. Let's examine each of these techniques in more detail:
1. Rathering
Rathering involves creating a false dichotomy between two positions, implying that one must choose between them while ignoring the possibility of other alternatives or nuances. It often employs the word "rather" to suggest a stark contrast between two options, when in reality, the situation may be more complex. [1]
Example: Gould argues: "Change does not usually occur by imperceptibly gradual alteration of entire species but rather by isolation of small populations and their geologically instantaneous transformation into new species." [2]
Dennett points out that this passage presents a false dichotomy between gradualism and instantaneous transformation, implying that evolution must proceed in one way or the other. However, it is entirely possible for evolution to involve both gradual changes within populations and relatively rapid speciation events. [2]
Rathering can be a misleading technique because it oversimplifies complex issues and discourages consideration of alternative explanations.
2. Piling On
Piling on involves making a series of increasingly strong claims that build upon each other, culminating in a sweeping generalization that may not be fully supported by the evidence. The initial claims may be true or partially true, but the technique aims to create a sense of overwhelming momentum that leads the reader to accept the final, more dubious conclusion. [3]
Example: Gould states: "We talk about the 'march from monad to man' (old-style language again) as though evolution followed continuous pathways of progress along unbroken lineages. Nothing could be further from reality." [3]
Dennett notes that while Gould's ultimate point - that evolution is not a linear progression towards some predetermined goal - is valid, the way he phrases his argument is misleading. The phrase "nothing could be further from reality" suggests that the very idea of continuous lineages is entirely wrong, when in fact, it is a fundamental principle of evolutionary theory. Gould's use of "piling on" creates an exaggerated sense of contrast and downplays the importance of continuous lineages in evolution. [4]
Piling on can be problematic because it can lead to the acceptance of overstated or unsubstantiated claims based on the persuasive force of the argument's structure rather than the strength of the evidence.
3. The Gould Two-Step
The Gould Two-Step is a more elaborate rhetorical maneuver that involves two distinct stages: [5]
Creating a Straw Man: First, the arguer misrepresents their opponent's position, presenting a weaker or distorted version of their argument that is easier to attack.
Claiming Victory by Misinterpreting Concessions: Then, the arguer cites evidence that their opponent does not actually hold the straw-man view they have attributed to them. However, instead of acknowledging their misrepresentation, the arguer interprets these citations as concessions to their attack, implying that their opponent has been forced to back down from their original position.
Example: Dennett describes how Gould invented the term "extrapolationism" to describe a supposed error in Helena Cronin's book The Ant and the Peacock. Gould portrays this as a doctrine of extreme gradualism and continuity that is easily refuted by the fact of mass extinctions. However, Dennett argues that Cronin never actually endorsed such a view, and that Gould's characterization of her position is a straw man. [6]
The Gould Two-Step is a deceptive technique because it creates the illusion of winning an argument while avoiding genuine engagement with the opponent's actual views.
In summary, Dennett's "Three Species of Goulding" highlight the importance of being aware of rhetorical techniques that can be used to subtly bias an argument. While these techniques are not necessarily fallacious in themselves, they can be employed to mislead or misrepresent opposing positions. By identifying and understanding these techniques, readers can become more critical consumers of information and better equipped to navigate complex arguments.
